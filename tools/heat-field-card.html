Heat Survival Field Card

What OSHA, NIOSH, CDC, NOAA's National Weather Service, the US Army and heat-physiology research say about working in severe heat. This is the real-world data behind HS-03. Where DCW site rules are stricter, a DCW note says so. On site, follow HS-03 and your crew lead's posted heat level.

Risk scale
70–124°F = NWS heat index in shade. Full sun adds up to 15°F.
125–180°F = air temperature, dry air. These zones are past the NWS heat index chart.
95°F ≈ skin temp. 104°F core = heat stroke.

DCW Zone names match the HS-03 heat levels. Your crew lead posts the heat level and the WBGT. Follow whichever is more restrictive. If the crew lead's local WBGT is higher than the Ch 16 value, the local reading wins.

The numbers that matter

Physical limits

95°F

Roughly skin temperature. When the air is hotter, it adds heat to you. Only sweat evaporating takes heat away, and fans stop helping.

104°F

Core body temperature that marks heat stroke, along with confusion or collapse. Brain and organ damage starts here.

77–88°F

Wet-bulb limit measured in young, healthy adults doing light activity (Penn State, 2022): about 86–88°F in humid heat, but only about 77–82°F in hot, dry air like a desert. Above it, core temperature keeps rising. The older theoretical limit was 95°F.

48 oz/hr

About the most fluid the gut can absorb (1.5 qt). Heavy work in heat can sweat out more than that, so you fall behind no matter how much you drink.

2%

Body weight lost as sweat before strength, judgment and heat tolerance drop noticeably.

158°F

Bare metal this hot burns skin in about one second of contact (ISO 13732-1). Sun-baked metal, sand and pavement get there.

CDC / NIOSH

Heat illness, mild to fatal

Emergency

Heat stroke

Confusion, slurred speech, collapse, seizure, or very hot skin. They may still be sweating. If it could be heat stroke, treat it as heat stroke. Don't wait for a thermometer: after hard work in heat, only a rectal reading is reliable. Mouth, ear and forehead readings read low.

Cool first, transport second.

Korey Stringer Institute: when cold-water immersion starts right away and core temperature drops below about 102°F within 30 minutes of collapse, survival is close to 100%. Medics stop cooling at about 101–102°F.

  1. Radio Ch 1: “Emergency, emergency, emergency. Heat casualty”, then location (Sector-grid, structure, deck, landmark), number hurt, hazards and your call sign. Stay on Ch 1 until Hub Command or the medic releases you.
  2. Get them out of the heat into the cooling station. Take off extra clothing and gear.
  3. Cold-water immersion is the fastest method. On site, use the ice trough at the nearest cooling station: water up to the neck, head supported, water stirred.
  4. No trough? Douse them with cold water without stopping. Lay ice-water towels on them and swap each one for a freshly iced towel every 2–3 minutes. Put ice packs at the neck, armpits and groin. Fan only while the skin is soaking wet: in air over 95°F, fanning dry skin adds heat.
  5. Stay with them. Don't give fluids if they're confused or can't swallow. Keep cooling until the medic takes over.
US Army TB MED 507, April 2022, Table 3-2

Work / rest and water by WBGT

WBGT (wet-bulb globe temperature) combines heat, humidity, sun and wind. Each cell: minutes of work / minutes of rest each hour, then water per hour, for heat-acclimatized workers in light clothing.

WBGT °FLightModerateHeavyVery heavy

TB MED 507 calls the light column "Easy" (about 250 W); moderate is about 425 W, heavy 600 W, very heavy 800 W. Its examples: easy = weapon maintenance, drill; moderate = patrolling with a 30-lb load, digging a defensive position; heavy = patrolling with a 45-lb load, four-person litter carry, jogging 4 mph; very heavy = two-person litter carry, obstacle course. HS-03 gives site examples. No limit = no limit to work per hour, up to 4 continuous hours. The times and water amounts sustain at least 4 hours of work. Water needs vary by about ¼ qt per hour with the person and with full sun or full shade. Rest means minimal activity (sitting or standing), in shade if possible. Never drink more than 1½ qt per hour or 12 qt per day. Heavy protective clothing (chemical protective suits): add 10°F to the WBGT for easy work and 20°F for moderate and heavy work.

DCW The HS-03 table is stricter. It takes the stricter of the 2003 and 2022 editions in every cell: for example moderate work 50/10 at 82–84.9 (2022: no limit), light work 50/10 at 90+ (2022: no limit), heavy work 10/50 at 90+ (2022: 15/45). DCW also adds site-rule rows: at WBGT 92+, no heavy or very heavy work outside cooled spaces, and moderate work only with a cooling vest (no vest, no work); at 95+, essential work only. On site, use the HS-03 table.

OSHA / NIOSH

Hydration

  • 1 cup (8 oz) every 15–20 minutes while working in heat. Don't wait for thirst; it lags behind real losses.
  • No more than 48 oz an hour, or 12 qt a day. Drinking too much plain water can dilute blood sodium (hyponatremia). Signs are confusion, headache, nausea and swelling. Confused after drinking a lot? Don't give more water. Treat it as an emergency: only a medic can tell low blood sodium from heat stroke.
  • Sweating for more than 2 hours: add electrolytes, either a sports drink or food with your water. Avoid salt tablets (CDC/NIOSH, TB MED 507). DCW: no salt tablets unless a medic gives them.
  • Weigh in and out. Replace each pound lost with about 16–24 oz over the next few hours (sports-medicine guidance: 1.25–1.5 L per kg), within the hourly limit. Measure against that day's starting weight. Down more than 2%: stop heat work, cool off and rehydrate. DCW: pull off at 2% and check in at the FAS before going back.
  • Skip alcohol before shifts, and skip high-caffeine energy drinks during work.

NIOSH acclimatization schedule

Ease in. The first week kills.

OSHA: 50–70% of outdoor heat deaths happen in a worker's first few days on the job. Heat tolerance builds over 7–14 days and fades after a week or more away.

New workers: no more than 20% of a normal shift's heat exposure on day 1, adding no more than 20% a day. Experienced workers returning, or at the start of a heat wave: 50%, 60%, 80%, then 100% by day 4.

DCW Stricter (HS-03). Every reset restarts at Day 1 (20%), for every worker: after a fever, vomiting or diarrhea, any heat illness, or 7+ days away from heat work. Days 1–4 also use half the table's work minutes. “No limit” cells become 30/30 (site rule). Days 6–14: the crew lead checks every 15 min at WBGT 85+. A black-and-yellow RAMP band on the helmet marks Days 1–5.

OSHA hierarchy of controls

Protect from the top down

Engineering

Shade over the work area. Ventilation, AC or cooled break rooms. Reflective or insulated shields on hot equipment. Machines for heavy lifting. Misting only helps in dry air.

Administrative

Heavy work early or at night. Work/rest cycles and acclimatization. Buddy system and supervisor checks. Water stations close by. Training. A written emergency plan with the site address posted.

PPE

Cooling vests (ice, phase-change, or evaporative in dry air). Wide-brim hat or neck shade. Light, loose, breathable clothing. Reflective suits for radiant heat. Remember that heavy PPE adds heat load.

CDC / NIOSH

Who is at higher risk

  • New or returning workers, and anyone not acclimatized
  • Past heat illness, especially a previous heat stroke
  • Heavy or impermeable PPE: chemical suits, turnout gear, respirators
  • Illness: fever, vomiting, diarrhea, poor sleep, hangover
  • Medications: diuretics, antihistamines, beta-blockers, anticholinergics, some antidepressants and antipsychotics, stimulants
  • Health: heart disease, diabetes, kidney disease, obesity, low fitness, age 65+
  • Alcohol or drug use within the past day

NWS heat alerts: Heat Advisory, Extreme Heat Watch and Extreme Heat Warning. "Excessive Heat" was renamed "Extreme Heat" in March 2025.
