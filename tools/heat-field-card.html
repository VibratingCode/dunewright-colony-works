Worker reference · 70°F to 180°F

Heat Survival Field Card

What OSHA, NIOSH, CDC, NOAA's National Weather Service, the US Army and heat-physiology research say a worker needs to know to stay safe in severe heat. Find your temperature on the scale, read across, and follow the actions.

Risk scale
70–124°F = NWS heat index in shade. Full sun adds up to 15°F.
125–180°F = air temperature, dry air. These zones are past the NWS chart.
95°F ≈ skin temp. 104°F core = heat stroke.
The numbers that matter

Physical limits

95°F

Roughly skin temperature. When the air is hotter, it adds heat to you. Only sweat evaporating takes heat away, and fans stop helping.

104°F

Core body temperature that marks heat stroke, along with confusion or collapse. Brain and organ damage starts here.

88°F

Wet-bulb limit measured in young, healthy adults (Penn State, 2022). Above it, core temperature keeps rising even at rest. The older theoretical limit was 95°F.

48 oz/hr

About the most fluid the gut can absorb (1.5 qt). Heavy work in heat can sweat out more than that, so you fall behind no matter how much you drink.

2%

Body weight lost as sweat before strength, judgment and heat tolerance drop noticeably.

158°F

Surface temperature that burns skin in about one second of contact. Sun-baked metal, sand and pavement get there.

CDC / NIOSH

Heat illness, mild to fatal

Emergency

Heat stroke

Confusion, slurred speech, collapse, seizure, or very hot skin. They may still be sweating. If it could be heat stroke, treat it as heat stroke.

Cool first, transport second.

Korey Stringer Institute: nearly everyone survives when core temperature is brought under 104°F within 30 minutes.

  1. Call 911 or your site emergency number. Give the exact location.
  2. Get them out of the heat and into shade or a cool space. Take off extra clothing and gear.
  3. Cold-water immersion is the fastest method. Put them in a tub or tarp of ice water up to the neck, keeping the head supported.
  4. No tub? Soak them with cold water and lay ice-water towels all over the body. Put ice packs at the neck, armpits and groin, and fan hard.
  5. Stay with them. Don't give fluids if they're confused or can't swallow. Keep cooling until help takes over.
US Army TB MED 507

Work / rest and water by WBGT

WBGT (wet-bulb globe temperature) combines heat, humidity, sun and wind. Minutes of work / minutes of rest each hour, for acclimatized workers in light clothing.

WBGT °FEasy workModerateHard

Easy: walking on a hard surface, light hand work. Moderate: patrolling, light digging, carrying a light load. Hard: heavy digging, carrying heavy loads, climbing. Rest means sitting in shade. Never drink more than 1½ qt per hour or 12 qt per day.

OSHA / NIOSH

Hydration

  • 1 cup (8 oz) every 15–20 minutes while working in heat. Don't wait for thirst; it lags behind real losses.
  • No more than 48 oz an hour. Drinking too much plain water can dilute blood sodium (hyponatremia). Signs are confusion, headache, nausea and swelling.
  • Sweating for more than 2 hours: add electrolytes, either a sports drink or salty food with your water.
  • Weigh in and out. Every pound lost is about 16 oz to replace. Losing more than 2% of body weight means drink more and work less.
  • Skip alcohol before shifts, and skip high-caffeine energy drinks during work.

NIOSH acclimatization schedule

Ease in. The first week kills.

OSHA: 50–70% of outdoor heat deaths happen in a worker's first few days on the job. Heat tolerance builds over 7–14 days and fades after a week or more away.

New workers: no more than 20% of a normal shift's heat exposure on day 1, adding no more than 20% a day. Experienced workers returning, or at the start of a heat wave: 50%, 60%, 80%, then 100% by day 4.

OSHA hierarchy of controls

Protect from the top down

Engineering

Shade over the work area. Ventilation, AC or cooled break rooms. Reflective or insulated shields on hot equipment. Machines for heavy lifting. Misting only helps in dry air.

Administrative

Heavy work early or at night. Work/rest cycles and acclimatization. Buddy system and supervisor checks. Water stations close by. Training. A written emergency plan with the site address posted.

PPE

Cooling vests (ice, phase-change, or evaporative in dry air). Wide-brim hat or neck shade. Light, loose, breathable clothing. Reflective suits for radiant heat. Remember that heavy PPE adds heat load.

CDC / NIOSH

Who is at higher risk

  • New or returning workers, and anyone not acclimatized
  • Past heat illness, especially a previous heat stroke
  • Heavy or impermeable PPE: chemical suits, turnout gear, respirators
  • Illness: fever, vomiting, diarrhea, poor sleep, hangover
  • Medications: diuretics, antihistamines, beta-blockers, anticholinergics, some antidepressants and antipsychotics, stimulants
  • Health: heart disease, diabetes, kidney disease, obesity, low fitness, age 65+
  • Alcohol or drug use within the past day

NWS heat alerts: Heat Advisory, Extreme Heat Watch and Extreme Heat Warning. "Excessive Heat" was renamed "Extreme Heat" in March 2025.
