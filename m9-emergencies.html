When it goes wrong.

Over 15,000 people on one site means emergencies will happen. What you do in the first five minutes decides who lives.

Know the sirens

Site-wide · Rebroadcast on Ch 1
Strobes in sealed cabs, loud areas & shelters
Take shelterSteady tone · 3 min · repeats every 5 min · red strobes

Sandstorm or other air hazard. Make safe and go to the nearest sealed shelter now. It repeats while the danger lasts.

Evacuate to musterAlternating high-low · 3 min · repeated · amber strobes

Stop work, make your equipment safe and walk to your muster point.

All stop3 short blasts · 1 second each

Stop all motion, hot work and machine operation. Hold position and listen to Ch 1. A suspended load is landed only on the signal person's direction.

All clearOne 15-second tone · then voice on Ch 1

Hub Command says on Ch 1: "All clear, all clear, [area]." Never leave a shelter or restart work on a tone alone. Wait for the Ch 1 voice all clear or your crew lead.

Weekly siren test: every 7th day at 1200, announced on Ch 1 before and after ("This is a test"). Machine horns are machine-local, never site signals: 1 = stopping, 2 = moving forward, 3 = backing up. Full radio rules: REF-RAD.

Weather warnings

Atmos on Ch 16 · Hub Command rebroadcasts on Ch 1

Sandstorm watch

A storm is possible within 2 hours.

Secure loose material. Plan how loads come down. Stage crews near shelter.

Sandstorm warning

Arrival expected within 30 minutes.

Land all loads. Booms down (tower cranes to free-slew per maker). Climbers and MEWPs (aerial lifts) down. Move to shelter. The take-shelter siren sounds no later than 15 minutes before arrival.

Flood warning

Called by grid on Ch 1 and Ch 16.

Leave wadis, trenches and low cuts at once. Climb to high ground. There is no flood siren.

Lightning alert

Strikes within 10 miles.

Booms down, off steel, out of MEWPs. Get into a sealed shelter or a closed vehicle, never under steel. Resume 30 minutes after the last strike within 10 miles.

Every radio scans Ch 1 + Ch 16 + your sector channel. Warnings that have no siren reach you only by radio, so keep your scan on. Wind limits: HS-06. Aerial lifts: Supplement C (HS-SC).

The emergency call

Channel 1. Speak slowly. Plain language. No codes.

"Emergency, emergency, emergency.
[type] Fall, worker suspended.
[location] Charlie-14, Tower B, Deck 3, east stair.
[hurt] Two hurt, one not moving.
[hazards] Loose steel overhead.
[you] This is Charlie Crew 14-3."

Types: Heat casualty · Man down · Fall · Fire · Gas · Chemical · Structural · Struck-by · Caught-in · Electrical · Confined space · Flood · Sandstorm. A short qualifier after the type is fine. A burn with nothing still burning is "Man down".

Location: sector-grid, structure, deck, landmark.

After the call: the caller stays on Ch 1 until Hub Command or the medic releases them. Everyone else stays off Ch 1 (and off Ch 14 during a mass casualty). Other channels keep working. Workers use Ch 14 only when told.

Response targets (site rule): a FAS medic on scene within 4 minutes anywhere in the sector. Fire & Rescue within 10 minutes. Start first aid until they arrive.

First on scene? You're in command.

  • You are the incident commander until someone relieves you. Your jobs: call it in, keep people out of danger, start first aid.
  • Make it safe for you first. Stop equipment, kill power, look up for loads. A second casualty helps nobody.
  • Give jobs by name. "You, call it in. You, get the trauma kit and the AED. You, keep people back."
  • Who takes over: Fire & Rescue takes command of fire, rescue and hazmat (gas or chemical) scenes when they arrive. Otherwise the most senior supervisor on scene takes over, or whoever Hub Command names. The Super runs muster and headcount.
  • Hand off out loud. "Rescue 3, you have command. Two hurt, one under the steel, power is off." The new commander says "I have command."
  • Write down times: when it happened, when the tourniquet went on, when the medic arrived.
  • Red helmets are Fire & Rescue (call sign "Rescue [n]"). Medics wear red with a white cross. Do what they say. A red FIRE WATCH vest carries no rescue or command authority.

Stop the bleed

Someone can bleed to death in under five minutes. You are faster than any medic.

Pressure. Pack.
Tourniquet.

Every crew carries a trauma kit with two tourniquets, and a first aid kit. Every crew has a first aider trained in CPR and Stop the Bleed. Know who it is and where the kits are.

  1. Gloves on if you have them. Find the bleeding. Cut or open clothing so you can see the wound.
  2. Push hard with both hands, straight down on the wound. Keep pushing.
  3. Arm or leg bleeding that spurts, pools or soaks through? Tourniquet now, 2–3 inches above the wound, never on a joint. Tighten until the bleeding stops. It will hurt. That's right. Still bleeding? Put a second tourniquet above the first.
  4. Deep wound in the neck, armpit or groin? Pack it full of gauze or clean cloth. Push hard on top for at least 3 minutes, then keep pushing.
  5. Write the time on the tourniquet or their forehead. Never loosen it. Medics take it off.

CPR & AED

100–120Pushes a minute
2–2.4 inDeep, adults
  1. Not responding and not breathing normally? Call it on Ch 1 and send someone for the AED.
  2. Casualty on hot steel or sand? Slide a mat under them fast.
  3. Heel of your hand in the center of the chest. Push hard and fast. Let the chest come all the way back up.
  4. Turn on the AED (automated external defibrillator) and do what it says. Keep going until a medic takes over.
  5. Swap pushers every 2 minutes. Tired hands push too shallow.

On a hot site, anyone who collapses is a heat casualty until a medic says otherwise. Call it as "Heat casualty". Others cool them while you push (HS-03).

AEDs are at every FAS (Forward Aid Station), shelter, structure cooling station and lift muster. No point on site is more than a 3-minute round trip from one. They're marked on the HS-01 site map. AED cabinets are cooled, because heat kills the battery and pads (site rule).

Fire

Fight it only if it's waste-bin size, your exit is behind you, and you have the right extinguisher. Not out in about 10 seconds? Leave and call Ch 1 "Fire". Never fight a polymer, power-cell or gas-fed fire. A polymer fire is a fire in bulk feedstock, a curing resin mass, a printed structure or panel stack, or a spray zone. A waste-bin-size fire in rags or scrap may be fought. Smother a rag can with its lid. A fire that's out: tell your foreman, who logs it on Ch 13.

Sandstorm: take shelter

  1. Warning on Ch 1 or Ch 16, or the steady siren: make safe and walk to the nearest sealed shelter. Don't run for your own sector.
  2. Wrap your face, goggles on. Keep a hand on your buddy or a rope line. Visibility can drop to nothing.
  3. The senior person in the shelter takes charge (the most senior supervisor there: white hat, then a crew lead).
  4. Airlock: one door open at a time. Keep bringing people in. Never lock anyone out.
  5. Arriving late? Bang three times on the outer door, or call the shelter on its sector channel. Step in. The outer door closes, then the inner door opens.
  6. Headcount. The senior person reports on that shelter's sector channel:
"Charlie Super, Shelter Charlie-14.
Twenty-three inside. Charlie Crew 14-3, eleven. Delta Crew 22-1, twelve. No injuries. Over."

Someone missing? Report it on Ch 1, with where they were last seen. Crew leads also tell their own foreman where their people are. Stay until the Ch 1 voice all clear. Don't open the door to look.

Inside the shelter

  • Stocked: water, ice, toilets, first aid kit, AED, radio, emergency lighting. The stock card by the inner door shows how long the water and ice last at full capacity.
  • Capacity is painted on the outer door (site rule). Full? Still let everyone in, and tell the Super on the sector channel so new arrivals go elsewhere.
  • Long storm: ration water and ice by the stock card. Sit, rest and keep the radio on. Buddies check each other for heat illness. Report low stock to the Super before it runs out.
  • Air and heat: watch the temperature and CO₂ readout by the inner door (site rule). Report rising readings on the sector channel.
  • Power, cooling or seal fails: keep both doors shut, because the storm is worse. Emergency lights on. Report it on Ch 1 at once. Everyone sits still, sips water and puts ice at the neck. Dust getting in? Wet cloth over your face, goggles on. Anyone sick: Ch 1 "Heat casualty". Stay put unless Fire & Rescue orders a move.
  • Flood warning at the same time? Never shelter in a wadi, trench or low cut. Lightning: sealed shelter or closed vehicle, never under steel.

Evacuate & muster

  1. Alternating siren: stop and make safe. Loads land on the signal person's direction. Park machines. Close valves and torches. Never go back for tools.
  2. Walk to the assigned muster point of the sector you're working in today (MP-A1, MP-A2 … MP-H2). Use the assigned route. Route blocked? Use that sector's other muster point.
  3. Gas, smoke or chemical release: move upwind and uphill. Don't walk through the cloud. If your muster point is downwind, command names another one on Ch 1.
  4. Report to your crew lead. Your crew lead reports in person to the muster warden (a named crew lead at each muster point). The warden reports once to the Super on the sector channel:
"Charlie Super, Muster MP-C1.
Fourteen crews. One hundred sixty-two assigned, one hundred sixty present. Two missing, last seen Charlie-14, Tower B, Deck 3. No injuries. Over."
  • Crew leads also tell their own department where their people are.
  • Away from your crew? Go to the nearest muster point of the sector you're in. Give the warden your name, Colonist ID and crew.
  • Missing people: Rescue searches. You report where they were last seen.
  • Drills: every sector runs a shelter drill and an evacuation drill each month. Safety & Health keeps the records. Treat every drill as real.

Flash flood

Rare rain upstream fills dry channels in minutes, even under a clear sky over you.

  • Flood warning by grid on Ch 1 and Ch 16: leave wadis, trenches and low cuts at once.
  • Climb. Higher ground beats faster ground.
  • There is no flood siren. Keep Ch 1 and Ch 16 on scan.
  • Never walk or drive into moving water. Turn around, don't drown.

Mass casualty: stay on your crew

  • Don't self-dispatch. A thousand helpers arriving at once block the medics, the cranes and the road.
  • Stay with your crew and your crew lead. Your lead will be told where you're needed.
  • Crews get roles: litter carriers, scene security, runners, triage helpers, traffic control for ambulances.
  • Stay off Ch 1 and Ch 14. Only the people working the incident talk there. Other channels keep working.
  • Walking wounded go to the FAS. Central Med at the Hub takes reds first.

After it's over

Report it. Report every injury, illness, first aid case, near miss, damage and unsafe condition to your crew lead before the end of the shift (immediately for anything serious), on form HS-IR with your Colonist ID. Don't move anything except to rescue. Safety & Health investigates with the crew. Findings are shared at muster. No retaliation.

Look after each other. Everyone involved gets a medical check and a debrief, plus a check-in within 72 h from an SRU counselor, Central Med or a peer supporter (site rule). Crews directly involved stand down from safety-critical roles for the rest of that shift (site rule). It's normal to be shaken. Poor sleep, replaying it, low mood and a short temper can start days later. Ask your crew lead or the FAS for critical incident stress support from Central Med. Asking for help carries no penalty.

Look before you leap

Make the scene safe before you go in. Stop the machine, kill the power, look up for loads. Gas, chemical or confined space? Don't go in. Call it in and wait for Rescue with breathing air (Supplement A (HS-SA)). Rescuers who rush in become the next casualties.

Stop-work authority

Anyone can stop unsafe work. No penalty, no questions. Stop it, make it safe, tell your crew lead (your foreman if there's no crew lead; any green hat if it isn't fixed). Work restarts only when the crew lead confirms the fix with the person who stopped it. Every stop is logged.
