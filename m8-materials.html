Read the label.

The polymers, resins and coatings that build this city can blind you, burn you or ruin your lungs for life.

Sensitized?

Some chemicals only give you one warning.

Isocyanates (spray foams, polyurethane coatings, QuickSet C-4X) and epoxy resins can make your body allergic to them. It can happen after one big exposure or after months of small ones. Once you are sensitized, even a trace can set off an asthma attack or a skin reaction. It does not go away.

Report wheezing, chest tightness, a cough at night, or an itchy rash on your hands or face to the Forward Aid Station (FAS) the same shift. Symptoms can start hours later, even at night in barracks. Report them anyway. Sensitized workers are moved off that material for good.

The 9 hazard symbols

GHS (Globally Harmonized System) pictograms · on every label

Red diamond border, black symbol, white background. No label? Don't touch or move it. Keep people back. Leaking or fuming: get everyone out upwind and call Ch 1 "Emergency, emergency, emergency. Chemical." yourself. Otherwise tell your foreman, who calls Ch 15 Quartermaster.

Danger

The more severe hazards. Can kill, blind, cause cancer, or burn on contact.

Warning

Less severe, still harmful. Skin or eye irritation, harmful if swallowed, can make you drowsy, suspected of causing cancer.

Every label carries one signal word, never both. A supplier label shows the product name, signal word, pictograms, hazard statements ("Causes severe skin burns"), precautions ("Wear protective gloves"), and the supplier's name, address and phone number.

The fire diamond

  • Blue · Health
  • Red · Flammability
  • Yellow · Instability (reactivity)
  • White · Special: W = reacts with water, OX = oxidizer, SA = simple asphyxiant gas
  • 0 = minimal, 4 = severe. Example: QuickSet C-4X hardener (Health 3, Flammability 1, Instability 1, W = reacts with water).

Careful: the SDS runs the other way. In SDS Section 2, Category 1 is the WORST and higher numbers are milder. On the diamond, 4 is the worst. The diamond is for Fire & Rescue. For your own work, go by the label and the SDS.

Fire & Rescue reads this diamond from 50 ft away. Keep it visible on every store and tank.

Site materials

Hazard cards · full SDS at the chem store
Printed structural polymer

FerroLattice-7

Health hazardExclamation mark

Extruded at about 500°F by the structure printers. Printing releases ultrafine particles and irritating fumes. The bead stays hot enough to burn for minutes after it looks set.

PPE: heat gloves, face shield at the head, half-mask with P100 + organic vapor cartridges inside print enclosures. Printer exhaust on before the head heats. Going inside the printer barrier: lock out first (HS-07).

Epoxy-isocyanate fast-cure resin

QuickSet C-4X

CorrosionHealth hazardExclamation mark

Causes allergic skin rash and asthma. Mixing makes heat. A large mixed batch can run away, smoke, and ignite. Desert heat shortens pot life. The hardener reacts with water: keep it sealed and dry.

PPE: nitrile gloves (double), goggles, sleeves. Spraying: supplied-air respirator only (site rule). Brushing or rolling in open air: full-face respirator, organic vapor + P100 cartridges, new every shift (site rule). Eyewash right beside the mixing point.

Leftover mix: mix only the pot size on the label. Pour leftovers out thin (under ½ in) on the sand in the cure tray, in the shade, at least 10 ft from anything that burns (site rule). Leave it until it is hard and cool, then bin it. Never put mixed resin in a closed container, a bin, or the sun.

Nano-ceramic heat-shield spray

VeilCoat

FlameHealth hazardExclamation mark

Sprayed on hulls and roofs to reflect sun. The solvent carrier is flammable. Spray mist carries nanoparticles deep into the lungs.

PPE: full-face respirator with P100 + organic vapor cartridges, coveralls, gloves.

Hot work: none within 35 ft while spraying. Hot work starts again only after a gas test reads below 1% LEL at the work spot and in any low or enclosed space nearby (site rule), and the PTW-HW is signed again (HS-05). Spraying inside a hull, tank or other confined space needs a PTW-CS: Supplement A (HS-SA).

Alkaline sand-polymer binder

Dunecrete

CorrosionHealth hazard

Strongly alkaline when wet, like cement. Causes chemical burns that start painless and get worse for hours. Dry mix and cutting cured Dunecrete make silica dust (HS-02).

PPE: alkali-resistant gloves and boots, goggles, long sleeves. Wet cutting or vacuum shrouds only. Wash skin contact with water right away. Eyewash right beside every mixing point and pour.

Old Earth hazards that still apply

Real chemistry behind the site materials

Isocyanates

In spray foams, polyurethane coatings and C-4X. A leading cause of work-related asthma. They harm you at levels you can't smell. Spray only in supplied air (site rule). Keep bystanders out of the marked spray zone.

Epoxy resins

Uncured resin and hardener cause allergic dermatitis. It starts as a red, itchy rash on hands and wrists. Never wipe resin off skin with solvent. Use soap and water.

Curing heat

Resins give off heat as they cure. Mass speeds it up. A bucket of mixed resin can reach smoking temperature. Never leave mixed batches in a closed container or a trash bin.

Styrene

Vapor from polyester and vinyl ester resins. Headache, dizziness, eye and throat irritation. Styrene vapor burns. It can catch fire from about 88°F, so on most days here open resin gives off vapor that can ignite. No sparks, flames or hot work near open resin. Lids on. Fans rated for flammable vapor. Organic vapor cartridges in tight spaces.

Long term: probably causes cancer (IARC), and adds to hearing damage from noise.

Fiber dust

Cutting or sanding carbon fiber and fiberglass throws fine fibers and resin dust. They irritate skin, eyes and lungs. Wet cut or vacuum shroud, P100 respirator, sealed goggles.

Carbon fiber dust conducts electricity. Keep it out of panels, drives and printers. Clean up with a HEPA-filter vacuum. Never blow it around with air.

Burning polymers

Polyurethane smoke releases hydrogen cyanide. PVC releases hydrogen chloride. All of them make carbon monoxide. Never fight a polymer fire (bulk feedstock, a curing resin mass, a printed structure or panel stack, a spray zone). Get out, stay upwind, and call Ch 1 "Fire".

Respirators for spray and resin work

  • Industrial Hygiene runs the respirator program. You get a respirator only after the medical evaluation and fit test (HS-02). IH names the respirator for each job and sets the cartridge change schedule.
  • Spraying any isocyanate product (C-4X, spray foam, polyurethane coatings): supplied-air respirator, full-face or hood (site rule).
  • Spray permit (PTW-SP) for all isocyanate and VeilCoat spraying: Industrial Hygiene sets the respirator and the spray zone, and the area foreman signs. One location, one shift (site rule). See REF-PTW.
  • Supplied air is Grade D breathing air. Compressor intakes stay away from sand, dust and engine exhaust.
  • Brushing or rolling C-4X in open air: full-face respirator, organic vapor + P100 cartridges. New cartridges every shift (site rule), sooner if IH's schedule says so. Heat shortens cartridge life.
  • Never wait to smell it. Isocyanates harm you below the level you can smell.

Medical checks (site rule)

  • Before your first job with isocyanates or epoxies, and every year after: a breathing questionnaire, a lung function test and a skin check.
  • Industrial Hygiene and Central Med run the checks. Your results stay private. Your foreman only learns whether you are cleared.
  • Report symptoms any time, between checks too.
  • Sensitized? You are moved off that material for good, with no penalty.

Eyewash & drench showers

  • Within 10 seconds of every chemical hazard, on the same level, with a clear path. Right beside the work for corrosives like Dunecrete and C-4X hardener.
  • Water must be lukewarm, 60–100°F. Units are shaded and insulated or chilled (site rule).
  • Before you open a container, run the eyewash and feel the water. Too hot or too cold? Report it. No work with corrosives until it's fixed.
  • Every unit is run weekly (date on its tag) and inspected yearly.
  • Keep the path and the unit clear. Never stack stock in front of it.

Every shift

  • Check the label and the SDS before you open anything new to you.
  • Wear the PPE in SDS Section 8. Not the PPE you happen to have.
  • Cap it when you're not pouring. Open containers off-gas in the heat.
  • Label every secondary container with the product name and its hazards, in words or symbols (for example "VeilCoat: flammable, lung hazard"). Never use a food or water bottle.
  • Pouring solvent or VeilCoat from a drum? Bond and ground the drum and the can. Dry air builds static fast.
  • Wash hands before eating, drinking or using the latrine. Resin on your hands ends up on your face.

Splash

10 seconds to water.
15 minutes under it.

Know where your eyewash and drench shower are before you open a container.

Example call: "Emergency, emergency, emergency. Chemical. Golf-6, Polymer Works, Deck 0, mixing bay. One hurt. C-4X hardener splash to the eyes. Golf Crew 12-3 Four."

  1. Eyes: get to the eyewash. Hold your eyelids open and roll your eyes. Flush at least 15 minutes at the station. Take out contact lenses while you flush. The medic comes to you.
  2. Skin or clothing: under the drench shower. Pull off contaminated clothing while the water runs. Flush 15 minutes.
  3. Breathed it in: get to fresh air, upwind. Don't go back in for anyone. Fire & Rescue goes in with breathing air.
  4. Ch 1: "Emergency, emergency, emergency. Chemical." Then location, number hurt, hazards (name the material), your call sign. Stay on Ch 1 until Hub Command or the medic releases you. Hub Command alerts the medics. Don't switch to Ch 14 unless you are told to.
  5. Helpers: gloves on before you touch them. Bag their clothes. Tell the medic what the chemical is before they touch the casualty, and hand over the SDS or the label. Section 4 is first aid.
  6. Alkali in the eye (Dunecrete, cement): keep flushing at the station until the medic says stop. The medic comes to you.

Spills

Spill kits sit at every Polymer Works station and every chem store (site rule). Know where yours is.

  • Small spill, you handle it: you know what it is, you're trained, the kit is at your station, you're wearing the PPE from SDS Section 8, one person can handle it, and it's under 5 gal (site rule). Contain it, soak it up, bag it, label it, tell your foreman. Your foreman restocks the kit through the Quartermaster on Ch 15.
  • Anything else (over 5 gal, unknown, fuming, going into a drain, someone hurt, or you're not sure): get everyone out, upwind. Ch 1: "Emergency, emergency, emergency. Chemical." Fire & Rescue's hazmat team handles it and takes command.
  • Isocyanate spill: cover it with absorbent, then the neutralizer from the kit. Leave the waste drum lid loose. It makes gas.

Hazardous waste

  • Never mix waste types. Every waste container is labeled with what's in it.
  • Resin: leftover mix cures in the cure tray first. Empty mixing buckets, cups and sticks stay open in the shaded cure area until hard and cool, then go in the cured-resin bin.
  • Solvent and VeilCoat rags: covered metal rag can, emptied every shift.
  • Isocyanate waste and used neutralizer: open-top drum, lid loose, until the chem store collects it.
  • Used gloves, suits and cartridges: bagged and labeled.
  • Full containers go back to your sector chem store. Never in general trash, a drain or the sand.

Fighting a fire

Fight it only if it's waste-bin size, your exit is behind you, and you have the right extinguisher. If it isn't out in about 10 seconds, leave and call Ch 1 "Fire". Never fight a polymer, power-cell or gas-fed fire. A polymer fire is a fire in bulk feedstock, a curing resin mass, a printed structure or panel stack, or a spray zone. A waste-bin-size fire in rags or scrap may be fought. Smother a rag can with its lid. A fire that's out: tell your foreman, who logs it on Ch 13.

Resin, polymer and coating smoke carries cyanide and CO: get out upwind. SDS Section 5 names the right extinguisher. Never use water on a water-reactive (W).

Unknown? Don't touch.

No label, a leaking drum, a smell you don't recognize, or a material you haven't been trained on: don't touch or move it. Keep people back. Leaking or fuming: get everyone out upwind and call Ch 1 "Emergency, emergency, emergency. Chemical." yourself, as for any spill you can't handle. Otherwise tell your foreman, who calls Ch 15.

Report it

Report every injury, illness, first aid case, near miss, damage and unsafe condition to your crew lead before the end of the shift (immediately for anything serious), on form HS-IR with your Colonist ID. Don't move anything except to rescue. Safety & Health investigates with the crew; findings are shared at muster. No retaliation.

Every splash, spill and exposure counts, even with no symptoms.

Stop-work authority

Anyone can stop unsafe work. No penalty, no questions. Stop it, make it safe, tell your crew lead (your foreman if there's no crew lead; any green hat if it isn't fixed). Work restarts only when the crew lead confirms the fix with the person who stopped it. Every stop is logged.
