Sparks travel.

Welding and cutting: the permit, the fire watch, your eyes, your lungs and your cylinders.

No permit?

No arc. No flame. No exceptions.

Any welding, cutting, grinding, brazing or torch work outside a designated welding bay needs a hot work permit. The permit is signed by your foreman (white hat) and posted at the work spot. It is good for one location and one shift. Brown hats do the work. A red hat stands fire watch.

Hot work permit

In this order, every time
  1. Look for a way to avoid hot work. Can the part be bolted, cut cold or moved to a fab yard bay? If yes, do that.
  2. Clear 35 feet. Move every combustible at least 35 ft away. If it can't move, cover it with fire blankets or metal shields.
  3. Seal openings. Cover floor gaps, wall holes and deck seams within 35 ft. Sparks fall through and start fires on the deck below.
  4. Check the other side. Heat moves through walls, pipes and hull plate. Look and clear the far side of whatever you weld.
  5. Test the air in enclosed modules or near tanks. No hot work if flammable gas is above 10% of the lower explosive limit (LEL).
  6. Post the fire watch. Red hat, charged extinguisher in hand, on Ch 13, eyes on the sparks. Fire watch does no other job.
  7. Foreman signs and posts the permit at the spot. Call it in on Ch 13: "Hot work starting, Echo-22, Deck 4."
  8. After the last arc: fire watch stays 60 minutes. Then a final walk of the area, and the permit is closed out on Ch 13.

Old Earth rule (OSHA 1910.252, NFPA 51B) is a fire watch during work and at least 30 minutes after. DCW site rule is 60 minutes, because dry sand-sealed insulation and polymer panels smolder before they flame.

The 35-foot rule

  • Sparks and slag fly more than 35 ft, farther from a height and in wind.
  • Work from Deck 3 or above? Clear the area below too, or catch the slag with a fire blanket.
  • Wind over 20 mph or a blowing-dust advisory on Ch 16: outdoor hot work stops.

Fire watch duties

  • Red hard hat. Charged extinguisher in reach. Know where the next one is.
  • Watch the sparks, not the weld. Look where they land, including below and behind.
  • Stop the work the moment anything smokes or catches. You have the authority.
  • Fire you can't put out in 10 seconds: Ch 1, "Emergency, emergency, emergency. Fire," with sector-grid and deck. Get everyone out.
  • Stay 60 minutes after the last arc. Feel walls and panels near the work for heat before you leave.

Arc eye

Ultraviolet from the arc burns the surface of the eye. You feel nothing at first. Hours later: gritty, burning, watering eyes, light hurts.

Do: report to the Forward Aid Station. Don't rub. Bystanders get it too, so put up welding screens.

Fumes

Stainless steel gives off hexavalent chromium, a carcinogen. Old Earth limit: 5 µg/m³ over a shift. Manganese damages nerves.

Do: keep your head out of the plume. Use the fume extractor every time. Respirator when the permit says so.

Metal fume fever

Welding galvanized (zinc-coated) steel. Flu-like chills, fever, metal taste, aches, 4–12 hours after work.

Do: grind the coating off first, ventilate, respirator. Report it. It is a sign your exposure is too high.

Gas cylinders

  • Upright and chained or on a cart. Valve cap on whenever it isn't hooked up.
  • Oxygen stored at least 20 ft from fuel gas, or separated by a wall at least 5 ft high with a half-hour fire rating.
  • Acetylene never above 15 psig. Above that it can decompose and explode on its own.
  • Flashback arrestors on both hoses at the regulator.
  • Leak test with soapy water. Never with a flame.
  • Never blow off clothes or dust with oxygen. Oxygen-soaked cloth burns like a torch from one spark.
  • Keep cylinders out of direct sun on the deck. Shade racks only.

Shock

  • Desert sweat is your enemy here. Wet skin and damp leathers let current through you.
  • Change to dry gloves when they get damp. Carry spares.
  • Insulate yourself from the work: dry rubber mat, dry boards. Never sit or kneel on wet plate.
  • Inspect leads before every shift. Cracked insulation gets tagged out (see HS-07).
  • Never wrap leads around your body. Never change electrodes with bare hands.

Burns & fire

Stop. Drop.
Roll. Cool.

Leathers save you from sparks. Synthetic clothing under them melts into skin. Wear cotton or flame-resistant base layers only.

  • Clothing on fire: stop, drop and roll. Crew smother it with a fire blanket.
  • Burns: cool with clean running water for 20 minutes. No ice, butter or ointment.
  • Remove rings, watches and gear near the burn before it swells. Leave stuck cloth in place.
  • Large, deep or face burns: Ch 1, "Emergency, emergency, emergency. Fire," then Ch 14 for the medic.

Heat stack: do

  • Welding leathers plus desert heat doubles your heat load. Read HS-03 Heat.
  • Schedule welding for the night shift whenever you can.
  • Treat welding in leathers as heavy work on the WBGT table.
  • Wear a cooling vest under leathers when the heat level is Danger or higher.

Heat stack: don't

  • Don't weld inside a sun-baked module without testing the air and the temperature.
  • Don't skip rest breaks because "the bead's almost done."
  • Don't strip your leathers to cool off mid-job. Stop, step out, cool down, then suit back up.

Anyone can stop hot work

Smoke, smell, sparks landing where they shouldn't, no fire watch, or no permit posted: say "stop" and call Ch 13. The welder stops. No penalty, no questions.

Worker acknowledgement

I have read this poster. I know where the nearest extinguisher, fire blanket and eyewash are, and I will not start hot work without a posted permit and fire watch.
