Sparks travel.

Welding and cutting: the permit, the fire watch, your eyes, your lungs and your cylinders.

No permit?

No arc. No flame. No exceptions.

Any welding, cutting, gouging, grinding, brazing or torch work outside a designated welding bay in the Fabrication Yards needs a hot work permit (PTW-HW). The foreman responsible for the work area (white hat) signs it, never the person doing the hot work. It covers one location and one shift. It is posted at the job and on the permit board, and opened and closed on Ch 13 Hot Work & Fire Watch.

Brown hats do the work. A trained fire watch in a red FIRE WATCH vest watches the sparks.

PTW-HW is not the heat entry permit (PTW-HE) from HS-03. Hot work at 140–159°F needs both. All permits: REF-PTW. Hot-work crews scan Ch 1, Ch 16, their sector channel and Ch 13 (REF-RAD).

Hot work permit (PTW-HW)

In this order, every time

The foreman who signs walks the spot first and checks steps 2–7 in person. Only foremen with the hot-work permit endorsement on their badge sign.

  1. Avoid hot work if you can. Bolt it, cut it cold, or move the part to a welding bay in the Fabrication Yards.
  2. Clear 35 feet. Move every combustible at least 35 ft away. Can't move it? Cover it with listed welding blankets or pads rated for slag, or with metal shields.
  3. Seal openings and protect every level below. Cover floor gaps, wall holes and deck seams within 35 ft. Above Deck 0, sparks fall: clear or cover every level below where they can land.
  4. Check the other side. Heat moves through walls, pipes and hull plate. Look at and clear the far side of whatever you weld, cut or heat.
  5. Test the air at the work, in low spots, and in any enclosed module, tank or trench within 35 ft. Start only below 1% LEL (lower explosive limit, site rule). Where gas can build up, keep a meter running the whole job.
  6. Check wind and heat. Outdoor hot work only below 20 mph and with no blowing-dust advisory (site rule). Check the heat level and WBGT in the table below.
  7. Post the fire watch. Red FIRE WATCH vest, charged extinguisher at hand, on Ch 13, no other job. Post a second fire watch on any level below that the first can't see.
  8. Foreman signs and posts the permit at the job and on the permit board, then opens it on Ch 13: "Echo Foreman 7, hot work open, Echo-22, Tower A, Deck 4, north face. Fire Watch Echo 3 posted."
  9. After hot work ends (last arc, flame or spark), the fire watch stays 60 minutes. Then they report "watch complete" on Ch 13. The signing foreman walks the area and closes the permit on Ch 13.

Stop and re-permit for a new location, a new shift or crew, wind or heat past a limit, new combustibles delivered, or a gas reading of 1% LEL or more.

Old Earth rules: OSHA 1910.252 sets a fire watch of at least 30 minutes after hot work. NFPA 51B (2019 edition onward) sets at least 60 minutes, plus up to 3 more hours of fire monitoring if the permit signer decides. DCW uses the NFPA 51B 60 minutes. Polymer panels, insulation or sand-sealed seams within 35 ft? The signing foreman adds walk-through checks for up to 3 hours, logged on the permit (site rule).

The 35-foot rule

  • Sparks and slag fly more than 35 ft, farther from a height and in wind.
  • Above Deck 0, at any height, sparks fall to every level below. Clear or cover them, and post a second fire watch where the first can't see.
  • Cover what can't move with listed welding blankets or pads. Fire blankets are for clothing fires.
  • No refueling within 35 ft of hot work.

Fire watch duties

  • Red FIRE WATCH vest over your normal PPE, fire-watch endorsement on your badge. You have no rescue or command authority. Red hats (Fire & Rescue) do.
  • Charged extinguisher at hand, the right type for what can burn. Know where the next one is.
  • Watch the sparks, not the weld. Look where they land, including below, behind and through openings.
  • Protect your eyes: safety glasses with side shields plus the filter lens on the permit, or watch from behind a welding screen. Never look at the arc.
  • Stop the work the moment anything smokes or catches. You have the authority. Fire? Follow Fighting a fire.
  • Stay 60 minutes after hot work ends (last arc, flame or spark). Feel walls and panels near the work for heat. Then report "watch complete" on Ch 13.

Arc eye

Ultraviolet from the arc burns the surface of the eye. You feel nothing at first. Hours later: gritty, burning, watering eyes, light hurts.

Do: report to the Forward Aid Station (FAS). Don't rub. Bystanders get it too: put up welding screens, and helpers and the fire watch protect their eyes.

Chromium & manganese

Stainless steel and chrome coatings give off hexavalent chromium, a carcinogen. Old Earth limit: 5 µg/m³ averaged over 8 hours (action level 2.5). Manganese damages nerves.

Do: head out of the plume, fume extractor every time. Industrial Hygiene measures exposure on stainless and chrome jobs, and the permit lists the respirator. Respirators only after your medical evaluation and fit test.

Metal fume fever

Welding galvanized (zinc-coated) steel. Flu-like chills, fever, metal taste, aches, 4–12 hours after work.

Do: grind the coating off first, extract the fumes, wear the respirator. Report it to the FAS. It means your exposure is too high.

Polymers & solvents

Cutting or heating FerroLattice-7 or composite panels gives off toxic gas. Arc UV turns chlorinated degreaser vapor into phosgene.

Do: treat it as hot work, with extraction and the respirator on the permit (HS-08). Degreasers away from the arc. No hot work within 35 ft of resin or VeilCoat spraying (spray permit PTW-SP). Restart only after a gas test below 1% LEL and a re-signed permit.

Cylinders in the heat

  • Cylinders stay under 125°F. Store them only in DCW cooled cylinder shelters. Stage them on the deck only on insulated, shaded cylinder carts.
  • On the deck: insulated, shaded cylinder carts. Bring only what today's job needs. Back to the shelter at shift end.
  • Temperature strip on every cylinder. Over 125°F: don't use it, tag it, call Ch 13.
  • Extreme heat level (125°F and up): no oxy-fuel cylinders on open decks (site rule).
  • Cylinder hissing at the valve, top or base? A relief device or fusible plug has let go. Acetylene plugs melt at about 212°F. Don't touch it. Clear everyone out to at least 330 ft (100 m), upwind (Old Earth ERG Guide 116). Call Ch 1 "Gas". If it lights, leave it: never fight a gas-fed fire.

Handling cylinders

  • Upright and secured: chained, or in a cylinder cart. Valve cap on whenever the regulator is off.
  • Away from the work, so sparks, slag and flame can't reach them.
  • In storage (any cylinder not hooked up for today's job): oxygen at least 20 ft from fuel gas, oil and grease, or behind a noncombustible wall at least 5 ft high with a half-hour fire rating.
  • Hoisting: only in a rated cylinder cage or cradle, or on a pallet with the cylinders secured and caps on. Never with a lifting magnet or field rig, a choker sling, or by the valve cap.
  • Valves: open slowly, standing to the side of the regulator. Acetylene valve no more than 1½ turns, wrench left on it. Acetylene never above 15 psig.
  • Flashback arrestors on both hoses at the regulator. Leak test with leak-test solution, never a flame.
  • No oil or grease on oxygen fittings, regulators or gloves. Never blow off clothes or dust with oxygen, and never ventilate with it.
  • Breaks and shift end: close the cylinder valves and bleed the hoses.
  • Flashback (squeal or hiss inside the torch): close the torch oxygen valve, then the fuel valve, then both cylinder valves. Find the cause before you relight.

Hot work in tanks, modules and confined spaces

  • Two permits: PTW-CS (the entry supervisor signs) and PTW-HW (the area foreman signs). Attendant at the opening. Follow Supplement A (HS-SA).
  • Meter running at the work the whole job: oxygen, LEL and toxic gas. Hot work stops at 1% LEL (site rule). Everyone out at 10% LEL, or oxygen below 19.5% or above 23.5%.
  • Too much oxygen burns you. A leaking torch or hose makes the air oxygen-rich, and clothing then burns explosively.
  • Too little oxygen kills you. Shielding gas (argon, CO₂) pushes oxygen out and pools in low spots.
  • Cylinders and welding machines stay outside the space.
  • Every break: close the torch valves, shut the gas off at the cylinders outside, and pull the torch and hoses out of the space (site rule; Old Earth requires removal at shift end).
  • Forced ventilation with fresh air, plus local fume extraction, running the whole job. Never ventilate with oxygen. Can't keep the fumes down? The permit lists an air-line respirator.
  • Drums, tanks and pipes that held fuel, resin or solvent: no welding, cutting or heating until cleaned (or filled with water), vented and gas-tested below 1% LEL. Hollow part? Vent it before you heat it.

Shock & welding power

  • Wet skin and damp leathers let current through you. Desert sweat counts.
  • Change to dry gloves when they get damp. Carry spares. Never change electrodes with bare or wet hands.
  • Insulate yourself from the work: dry rubber mat or dry boards. Never sit or kneel on wet plate.
  • Inspect leads and holders before every shift. Cracked insulation: hang a yellow OUT OF SERVICE tag (HS-07).
  • Clamp the work (ground) lead on the piece, as close to the weld as you can. Never let welding current run through a crane hook, wire rope, chain, sling or harness. Gas or fuel pipes and conduit are never the return path.
  • Never wrap leads around your body. Leaving the job or moving the machine? Switch it off at the supply.
  • Machine power, grounding, cords and GFCI: Supplement B (HS-SB).
  • Someone shocked: kill the power first. Never touch them while they're on it. Ch 1 "Electrical".

Radiography & survey lasers

  • Weld radiography (QA/QC, grey hats) checks welds with X-ray or gamma sources. The radiographers rope off the area and post yellow-and-magenta radiation signs.
  • Never cross a radiation rope or sign, even if the area looks empty. Inside the rope when it goes up? Walk out at once and tell the radiographer and your crew lead.
  • Survey and alignment lasers (grey hats): only trained operators, carrying proof of qualification. Laser warning signs posted.
  • Never stare into the beam or aim it at a person. Shutter closed or laser off when not in use. Survey lasers are usually Class 2 or 3R. Class 3B or 4 needs a controlled area and laser eyewear.
  • Blowing dust scatters the beam. Stop laser work where you can, and keep people out of the beam path.

Fighting a fire

  • Fight it only if it's waste-bin size, your exit is behind you, and you have the right extinguisher.
  • Not out in about 10 seconds? Leave and call Ch 1: "Emergency, emergency, emergency. Fire," location, number hurt, hazards, your call sign. Stay on Ch 1 until Hub Command or the medic releases you.
  • Never fight a polymer, power-cell or gas-fed fire. Hose or regulator burning? Shut the cylinder valves only if you can reach them safely, then leave.
  • Polymer fire: a fire in bulk feedstock, a curing resin mass, a printed structure or panel stack, or a spray zone. A waste-bin-size fire in rags or scrap may be fought. Smother a rag can with its lid.
  • A fire that's out: tell your foreman, who logs it on Ch 13.

Fire prevention

  • Extinguisher classes: A = wood, paper, cloth, packing. B = fuel, solvent, resin, gas. C = live electrical. D = burning metal. Every hot-work job has a charged ABC dry-chemical extinguisher (at least 2A:10B:C, site rule). Burning metal fines: Class D only, because water or ABC can make them flare.
  • Where: at least one on every deck, by a stair, no more than a 100 ft walk from any point. A B-rated one (10B or more) within 50 ft of fuel or gas in use. Every machine cab carries one (site rule).
  • Monthly check, initialed on the tag: in place, pin and seal intact, gauge in the green, no damage, nozzle clear of sand. Serviced yearly. Used even a little? Out of service until recharged.
  • Refueling: engine off. Bond the nozzle or can to the tank before fuel flows, because dry air builds static fast. Fill cans on the ground, never in a truck bed.
  • Housekeeping: rubbish, packing and offcuts out every shift. Oily and solvent rags in a closed metal can, emptied every shift. Fuel, gas and solvent on the deck only in the amount the job needs, in approved containers. Exits and stairs clear.
  • Sealed habs and modules fill with smoke fast. Know both ways out before you start. Power cells charge only in marked charging areas (HS-SB).

Burns

Cool it.
20 minutes.

Leathers save you from sparks. Synthetic clothing under them melts into skin. Wear cotton or flame-resistant base layers only.

  • Clothing on fire: stop, drop and roll. Crew smother it with a fire blanket.
  • Cool every burn under cool running water for 20 minutes. No ice, butter or ointment. Don't burst blisters.
  • Remove rings, watches and gear near the burn before it swells. Leave stuck cloth in place. Cover loosely with cling film or a clean non-stick dressing.
  • Bigger than their palm, deep, on the face, hands, feet, groin or a joint, or any trouble breathing: Ch 1, "Emergency, emergency, emergency. Man down, burn," then location, number hurt, hazards, your call sign. Say "Fire" only if something is still burning, and "Electrical" for an electrical burn. Stay on Ch 1 until Hub Command or the medic releases you.
  • Smaller burns: cool, cover, and someone walks them to the FAS. Chemical burn: flush 15 minutes (HS-08).

Heat stack: do

  • Welding leathers plus desert heat stack up your heat load. Read HS-03 Heat.
  • Schedule welding for the night shift whenever you can.
  • Wear a cooling vest under leathers when the heat level is Danger or higher.
  • Drink 8 oz every 15–20 minutes, even under the hood.

Heat stack: don't

  • Don't weld inside a sun-baked module without testing the air and the temperature.
  • Don't skip rest breaks because "the bead's almost done."
  • Don't strip your leathers to cool off mid-job. Stop, step out, cool down, then suit back up.

Qualified hands only

  • Only welders with the hot-work endorsement on their badge strike an arc or light a torch.
  • Structural welds need a current DCW welder qualification to AWS D1.1.
  • Pre-stasis qualifications count only after a practical retest. Every welder passes a test plate in the Fabrication Yards before field work.
  • Fire watch: fire-watch endorsement, trained on extinguishers and on raising the alarm.
  • Permit signers: foremen with the hot-work permit endorsement. Endorsements are listed in REF-GL.

Report it

Report every injury, illness, first aid case, near miss, damage and unsafe condition to your crew lead before the end of the shift (immediately for anything serious), on form HS-IR with your Colonist ID. Don't move anything except to rescue. Safety & Health investigates with the crew, and findings are shared at muster. No retaliation.

Every burn goes to the FAS. A fire that's out: tell your foreman, who logs it on Ch 13.

Anyone can stop hot work

Smoke, smell, sparks landing where they shouldn't, no fire watch, no posted permit: say "stop" and the welder stops. Anyone can stop unsafe work. No penalty, no questions. Stop it, make it safe, tell your crew lead (your foreman if there's no crew lead; any green hat if it isn't fixed). Work restarts only when the crew lead confirms the fix with the person who stopped it. Every stop is logged.

Worker acknowledgement

I have read this poster. I know where the nearest extinguisher, fire blanket and eyewash are, and I will not start hot work without a posted permit and a fire watch.
